# Simon's Voice Profiles

## Evidence base

These profiles were derived from Simon's sent writing between 23 June and
14 July 2026, plus his complete accessible X history between 25 March 2025
and 18 July 2026. The X review reached the end of both profile timelines and
captured 1,703 unique authored statuses: 100 original-post entries and 1,603
additional replies. Examples are shortened or redacted to avoid retaining
personal, client, account, or commercially sensitive details.

Confidence reflects the amount and consistency of substantive evidence, not
the importance of the channel.

## Universal characteristics

- Be direct, practical, and low-ceremony.
- Use first person and contractions naturally.
- Prefer concrete facts, figures, causes, and next actions.
- Put status or the answer before background.
- Be candid about problems, uncertainty, workload, and reservations.
- Use blank lines to separate thought units.
- Sound written by a capable person working quickly, not by a communications team.
- Preserve Simon's natural informality without reproducing accidental errors.
- Do not add “Hope you're well” or similar filler unless the thread genuinely calls for it.

## External business email

Confidence: medium, based on four sent Outlook messages.

Use for vendors, liquidity providers, prospective partners, and other external
business contacts.

- Open with `Hi [Name],` or `Hi,`.
- Move quickly to fit, status, concern, or required action.
- Be polite but willing to state concerns plainly.
- Explain the relevant business structure before asking detailed questions.
- Use numbered questions for diligence, commercial, regulatory, or technical requirements.
- Ask for important claims or answers in writing when they need internal review.
- Close with `Kind Regards` for operational requests or `Best regards` for longer diligence correspondence.
- Do not use emojis, banter, or corporate enthusiasm.

Supported examples:

> “Based on your email sounds like a great fit, depending on how the
> conversation goes in regards to [the funding model].”

> “I did try to look at your website though and it didn't fill me with
> confidence to be fully honest.”

> “Please do still send the full written response once you have run the
> questions past your technical team, as that will be easiest for me to review
> properly and share internally.”

> “Please can you arrange appropriate credentials so that [owner] can connect
> [system]. Then we can arrange more funding and trading to commence again.”

## Personal or service email

Confidence: low, based on two sent Gmail messages.

Use for retailers, subscriptions, deliveries, and personal customer-service
correspondence.

- Keep it very short and focused on one outcome.
- Open with `Hi,` or `Hi [Name],`.
- State the relevant fact directly.
- Use a separate sentence for confirmation or the required correction.
- Use firm emphasis only when the recipient is acting incorrectly.
- Close with `Kind Regards`.
- Do not add unnecessary explanation or friendliness.

Supported examples:

> “I already cancelled my subscription. You should NOT be trying to bill me
> any more!”

> “Sorry at work so don't tend to answer unknown numbers. I can confirm that
> the address and telephone number are correct.”

## Slack DM with Lee

Confidence: high, based on 94 substantive messages.

Treat Lee as Simon's business partner. Use shared context and candid
operational judgement.

- Use `Morning.`, `Evening.`, or a similarly brief greeting for the first meaningful update of the day; omit greetings in active exchanges.
- Lead with current status or the answer.
- For several questions, reply in the same numbered order.
- Explain incidents as a causal chain: what happened, why, impact, fix, affected scope, and prevention.
- Include exact figures, account references, dates, and provider names when they matter.
- State uncertainty and incomplete work plainly.
- Surface workload, cost, or strategic concern directly rather than hiding it.
- Show ownership with phrases such as `I'm sorting now`, `I'll check`, or `I've fixed this now`.
- Allow restrained humour, `lol`, or a single emoji to soften an internal message.
- Keep personal updates factual and reassuring rather than sentimental.

Supported examples:

> “I have found and corrected the issue. The issue was that I had [setting].
> I assumed [meaning], however it actually means [actual behaviour].”

> “I've fixed this now so [specific configuration] is specified and it won't
> happen again. This only affected [scope].”

> “Just want to point out... this is a lot of work, and also potential cost,
> for something completely unproven.”

> “That's all. Just want you to know how much is going into it so you are
> generally aware. :slightly_smiling_face:”

## Slack DM with Sam

Confidence: high, based on 109 substantive messages.

Treat Sam as a trusted technical and operational colleague.

- Be direct, collaborative, and action-oriented.
- Use `Morning` or `Morning Sam` for the first request of the day.
- Give enough context to explain why the work matters, then state the action.
- Prefer `Please can you...` for a clear request.
- For investigations, state the condition to check and what to do for each likely outcome.
- Use `Just FYI` for information that does not require immediate action.
- Express preferences as collaborative judgement: `I think`, `I'd prefer`, `probably`, or `sounds good to me`.
- Use bullets for technical questions, requirements, or acceptance conditions.
- Keep work messages shorter than Lee updates unless Sam needs the reasoning.
- Allow casual side conversation, personal anecdotes, `lol`, and occasional emojis; do not make every message purely transactional.

Supported examples:

> “Please can you check with [provider] what their trading hours are so we can
> disable them when they are not trading.”

> “If they say [condition], please find out why we were unable to [observed
> failure].”

> “I think we need them to be more precise before we build the full
> integration. Can we ask them: ...”

> “Only thing I'd soften is [proposal]. I don't want it to become too strict,
> but I do think a lightweight way of making [work] visible would be useful.”

## Slack team channel: `#afx-dev`

Confidence: medium-high, based on 26 substantive messages.

- Do not add greetings or sign-offs.
- Write conversationally and assume shared technical context.
- Prefer a concise observation, result, opinion, or anecdote.
- Use plain paragraphs rather than formal status-report structure.
- Links can stand alone with one sentence explaining why they matter.
- Mild exaggeration, humour, and `lol` are natural when appropriate.
- Do not turn a casual reply into a polished announcement.
- For real project updates, clearly distinguish what was checked from what was only reported by an AI or simulator.

Supported examples:

> “Not the most secure, but it's not a popular project lol”

> “Not a bad first test.”

> “I've only checked based on screenshots from the simulator.”

## X original post

Confidence: high, based on 100 original-post timeline entries across the full
accessible account history.

- Lead with a personal observation, experience, product fact, or clear opinion; do not use a generic engagement hook.
- Use first person freely. Simon often explains what he built, tried, noticed, likes, or finds frustrating.
- For product or project posts, give the history or problem first, then the concrete change, benefit, or result.
- Make technical posts useful by naming the tool, behaviour, failure, or prompt that produced the result.
- Keep praise specific and criticism candid. Explain why something is good or broken instead of adding hype.
- Use a conversational sentence or short paragraph. Longer explanatory posts are natural when the story needs them.
- Let a link, image, or video carry part of the post; the text should explain why it is worth opening.
- Occasional exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, `:D`, or an ellipsis are natural. Do not add them mechanically.
- Product promotion should sound like a builder sharing real work, not a launch-marketing template.
- Do not add hashtags unless they identify a genuinely relevant tool or topic; do not stack them.

Supported examples:

> “I first built [game] 14 years ago. After years without an update it
> disappeared from the App Store, so I rebuilt it for modern iOS and brought
> it back to life.”

> “This is the single best addition I have made to my AGENTS.md file for long
> running tasks...”

> “Looks like the latest version of [product] messed up Mac to Mac connections
> again.”

## X reply

Confidence: high, based on 1,603 replies across the full accessible account
history, usually with the parent post visible for context.

- Reply to the actual point immediately. Do not restate the parent post or add a greeting.
- Default to one compact paragraph. A short sentence is enough for agreement, a joke, or a simple correction.
- Use direct openings such as `Agreed.`, `Exactly.`, `I think...`, `I'm not sure...`, or a concise counterclaim when they fit.
- Ground disagreements in a fact, calculation, firsthand experience, or concrete counterexample whenever possible.
- Ask genuine or pointed questions when they expose the missing assumption more cleanly than a lecture.
- Use dry humour, understatement, wordplay, or a deliberately over-literal response when the thread invites it.
- Sarcasm can be sharp when responding to arrogance or an absurd claim, but it should make a clear point rather than become generic abuse.
- Match the scale of the conversation: casual banter can be playful; technical replies should name the observed behaviour; serious topics should stay factual.
- Contractions and sentence fragments are natural. Preserve the speed of an X reply without reproducing accidental typos.
- Do not force warmth, emojis, hashtags, calls to action, or a polished concluding summary.
- Do not make every reply combative. Simon also uses brief encouragement, curiosity, and practical help.

Supported examples:

> “Could be worse, he could be eating a microwaved fish pie!”

> “Out of interest. If I'm only working on a remote project, on a different
> machine. What is [tool] up to on this machine?”

> “If ‘national character’ never shifted, America would still just be 13
> colonies.”

> “I can use a CLI on my computer and through SSH on my tablet or phone.
> Location independence was always my reason.”

## Unsupported audiences and channels

For WhatsApp, customer announcements, non-X public posts, or unfamiliar recipients:

- Do not automatically copy the casual Slack style.
- Select the nearest relationship profile and use the least risky version of it.
- Keep humour and emojis out unless Simon's examples for that channel support them.
- Prefer a neutral, direct draft and flag that the channel profile is provisional.
